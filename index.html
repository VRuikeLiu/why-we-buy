I

The Land of Desire

The Roaring Twenties remade America — its cars, its music, its movies, its idea of pleasure. Henry Ford's assembly line and the new gospel of buying-on-credit put automobiles, telephones, and appliances into ordinary homes. Americans turned, as one historian put it, inward.

As historian Lawrence Glickman observed, Americans began “enjoying the private pleasures” of the era, cultivating an image of “indifference or cynicism to the well-being of the public sphere.” William Leach gave the decade its truest name: the United States had become the land of desire.

People craved goods, and producers raced to satisfy that craving. But beyond cheaper cars and easier credit, one force pushed consumer culture to a height it had never known — a revolution in the art of advertising. This is the story of how selling changed: from announcing what a product did, to whispering who you might become.

II

The Engines of Consumption

First, the machinery had to fall into place.

Henry Ford, the apostle of mass production, broke the automobile into stations along a moving line — each department building one part, the whole assembled at the end. Output soared as prices fell, until even working-class families bought Fords by the thousand.

Installment credit did the rest. Where families once saved for years, a down payment now unlocked the purchase. Crucially, by the mid-1920s, buying on credit had ceased to feel sinful — a quiet moral revolution. The nation's savings drained into its driveways.

III

The Age of Thrift

Before desire, there was discipline.

To understand how radical the change was, remember what came before it. Thrift was not merely a habit in pre-1920s America — it was a civic virtue, taught in classrooms and printed on government posters.

From Massachusetts in 1911 onward, “thrift education” became a curriculum requirement in state after state. Schools opened savings banks not for convenience but, in the words of one program, as “a developer of character.” And the children answered.

The Great War only deepened the creed. The U.S. Food Administration printed posters ordering citizens to “waste nothing.” A 1918 pamphlet, The Patriotism of Saving, condemned waste as “the selfish destruction by one of things which many need.” To save was patriotic. To spend was suspect.

And the advertisements of that earlier world sold exactly what you would expect: usefulness. Efficiency. Simplicity. Cleanliness. The product's job was to work — and the ad's job was to prove it.

Then advertising discovered the mind.

Advertisers in the 1920s grasped what their predecessors had missed: choice is emotional, often irrational. The trade abandoned what historian Roland Marchand called the “factory viewpoint” — the catalogue of what a product did — and turned instead toward “the mental processes of the consumer.”

They learned to manufacture feeling on purpose. They would warn you that not owning a product made you someone undesirable — and promise that owning it made you someone enviable. Strong emotion drowns out arithmetic. Usually, the feeling wins.

IV

The Fear — Identities You Could Lose

Buy this, or become someone lesser.

The first lever was dread. Advertisers attached three social humiliations to not owning their product — and assured you that even your closest friends would never warn you in time.

01 Losing your romantic attraction

02 A socially unrecognized appearance

03 Lacking taste

The arithmetic of dread: one act of reason for every twenty of emotion. With impulse in command, the buying rose.

VII

A National Ethos

Soon, Americans defined themselves by what they owned.

The change ran deeper than any single sale. From three directions at once — the pulpit, the parlor, and the President — consumption was reframed as identity itself.

IX

The Inheritance

Before the Twenties, thrift was a virtue and the ad sold a product's use. Within them, advertising learned to sell a self — and buying became the way Americans said who they were.

The decade ended in the Great Depression. But the machinery of desire it built never stopped running. The psychology is still in the advertisement; the identity is still in the purchase — well into our own century.
